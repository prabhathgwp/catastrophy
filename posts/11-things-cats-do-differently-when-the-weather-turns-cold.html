11 Things Cats Do Differently When The Weather Turns Cold

Photo by Gizem Gökce on Pexels — View on Pexels

As the leaves change color and the air turns crisp, cat owners often notice subtle yet distinct shifts in their feline companions' behavior. Just as we bundle up in sweaters and adjust our thermostats, cats have their own intricate ways of adapting to the dropping temperatures. Understanding these changes is key to ensuring your cat remains comfortable, healthy, and happy throughout the winter season. From seeking out the coziest spots in the house to subtle changes in their eating and sleeping habits, here are 11 things cats do differently when the weather turns cold.

1. Seeking Out Warmth

Perhaps the most obvious change is a cat's relentless pursuit of heat. When the temperature drops, a cat's natural instinct is to find the warmest available spot. You might find your cat sprawled across a sunbeam streaming through the window, curled up on top of the refrigerator (which emits a small amount of heat), or snuggled directly against a radiator or heating vent. This behavior is driven by their higher normal body temperature compared to humans; they feel the cold more acutely and are driven to conserve energy by staying warm.

Practical Tip: Create Warm Zones

Provide your cat with dedicated warm areas. A soft cat bed placed in a sunny corner, a heated pet bed, or even a cozy blanket draped over a favorite chair can give them a safe, warm retreat. If you have a sunny windowsill, ensure it is accessible and perhaps add a fleece blanket for extra comfort.

2. Increased Sleeping

It is very common for cats to sleep more during the winter months. While the average cat sleeps between 12 to 16 hours a day, you may notice this number creeping upward as the days get shorter. This increase in sleep can be attributed to a combination of factors: less daylight triggers a biological urge to conserve energy, and the simple comfort of staying curled up in a warm bed is irresistible when it's chilly outside.

Practical Tip: Respect Their Rest

While it's tempting to wake a sleeping cat, try to respect their need for extra rest. Ensure their sleeping area is quiet, draft-free, and comfortable. If you notice a sudden, drastic change in sleep patterns or lethargy that seems excessive, it's always worth a conversation with your veterinarian to rule out underlying health issues.

3. Changes in Appetite

Some cats experience a shift in their appetite when the weather turns cold. Similar to how humans might crave heartier meals in winter, some cats may eat more to generate body heat through digestion. Others, however, might become less interested in food, especially if they are spending less time outdoors and are generally less active. Monitoring your cat's weight and body condition is crucial during this time.

Practical Tip: Maintain a Consistent Diet

Stick to a regular feeding schedule and high-quality cat food. If you notice your cat eating significantly more, ensure they are getting enough exercise to prevent unwanted weight gain. Conversely, if their appetite wanes, warming up their wet food slightly can make it more aromatic and enticing, as cold food can sometimes be less appealing to a cat's sensitive nose.

4. Reduced Outdoor Exploration

For cats that have access to the outdoors, the cold often means a significant reduction in roaming. They may become more selective about when they go out, preferring the middle of the day when it's warmest, or they may stop venturing out altogether. Indoor cats may also show less interest in perching by the door or staring out the window at the snow.

Practical Tip: Safe Indoor Enrichment

If your cat is staying indoors more, it's vital to provide mental and physical stimulation. Puzzle feeders, interactive laser toys, and cat trees can help mimic the hunting and exploring they would normally do outside. This prevents boredom and helps maintain a healthy weight during the less active winter months.

5. Grooming Adjustments

You might notice your cat grooming themselves more frequently during the winter. While grooming is a year-round activity, the dry, cold air can lead to static electricity in their fur, which some cats try to alleviate by licking. Additionally, if your cat has a thicker winter coat, they may spend more time smoothing it down.

Practical Tip: Combat Dry Skin

The winter air can be harsh on a cat's skin, leading to dryness and flakiness. Regular brushing helps remove dead hair and distributes natural skin oils, which acts as a natural moisturizer. Consider using a humidifier in your home to add moisture to the air, which benefits both your cat's skin and your own.

6. Seeking Human Contact

Many cats become more affectionate and clingy when it gets cold. They may follow you from room to room more often, jump onto your lap more frequently, or insist on sleeping at the foot of your bed or even under the covers with you. This behavior is often a combination of seeking physical warmth from their favorite human and a desire for social security and comfort during the darker, colder months.

Practical Tip: Quality Bonding Time

Embrace these moments of increased affection. Gentle petting not only provides your cat with warmth but also strengthens the bond between you. However, always respect your cat's boundaries; if they push away, give them space, as some cats prefer to seek warmth independently.

7. Litter Box Habits

Cold weather can sometimes affect a cat's litter box habits. Some cats may be less inclined to go outside to relieve themselves if the ground is frozen or covered in snow. This can lead to holding it in for longer periods or, in some cases, accidents inside the house if the litter box is located in a cold area like a garage or porch.

Practical Tip: Warm and Accessible Litter

Ensure the litter box is located in a warm, dry, and easily accessible part of the house. If the box is in a cold area, consider moving it to a warmer room. Additionally, some cats prefer a litter with better clumping properties in winter, and keeping the box meticulously clean encourages consistent use.

8. Vocalization Changes

Some cats become more vocal during the winter. They might meow more frequently to get your attention to open a door, ask for food, or simply because they are feeling a bit stir-crazy from being indoors more often. The change in routine and reduced stimulation can lead to boredom-induced meowing.

Practical Tip: Enrichment and Interaction

Increase interactive play sessions to burn off excess energy and provide mental stimulation. Scheduled playtime with wand toys or feather teasers can reduce excessive vocalization by satisfying their predatory instincts in a safe, indoor environment.

9. Weight Fluctuations

Weight changes are a common concern during the winter transition. As mentioned, some cats eat more; others move less. This combination can lead to weight gain. Conversely, some cats may lose weight if they are very stressed by the change in environment or if underlying health issues are exacerbated by the cold (such as arthritis, which often worsens in damp, chilly weather).

Practical Tip: Monitor Body Condition

Regularly feel your cat's ribs and observe their waistline. If you are concerned about weight changes, consult your vet. They can determine if the changes are simply due to seasonality or if they indicate a medical issue that needs addressing.

10. Arthritis and Joint Stiffness

Just as many humans experience increased joint pain when the weather turns cold and damp, cats—especially senior cats—can suffer from arthritis. The cold can make joints stiffer and more painful, leading to reduced mobility. You might notice your cat jumping lower, hesitating to use the stairs, or being less playful than usual.

Practical Tip: Comfort for Aging Joints

Provide orthopedic beds that offer extra support. Ramps or steps can help them reach their favorite high spots without jumping. Discuss joint supplements like glucosamine and chondroitin with your veterinarian, and ensure they have a warm, soft place to rest that isn't on a cold tile floor.

11. Changes in Coat Texture

Many cats develop a thicker, denser winter coat to insulate themselves against the cold. This is a natural physiological response. You may notice the fur feeling softer or thicker to the touch. Shedding may also increase as they transition from their summer coat to their winter coat.

Practical Tip: Regular Deshedding

Regular brushing is essential during this coat transition. It helps remove the loose undercoat, prevents matting, and reduces the amount of fur your cat ingests while grooming, which can prevent hairballs. A deshedding tool can be particularly effective during the spring and autumn coat blows.

Feline Care FAQ

Q: Is it normal for my cat to sleep even more than usual in the winter?

Yes, it is very normal. Cats are crepuscular animals, meaning they are most active at dawn and dusk. During winter, the reduced daylight hours can trigger a biological instinct to conserve energy. As long as your cat is eating, drinking, and using the litter box normally, extra sleep is usually just a seasonal habit.

Q: My cat is shivering; should I be concerned?

Shivering is a sign that your cat is trying to generate body heat and may be too cold. Check for drafts in their sleeping area, ensure they have warm bedding, and feel their ears and paws—if these extremities are cold, they may need help warming up. However, if shivering is accompanied by lethargy or loss of appetite, contact your veterinarian immediately, as it could be a sign of hypothermia or illness.

Q: Can cats catch colds from the weather?

Cats cannot "catch a cold" in the same way humans do from exposure to cold weather alone. However, cold, damp conditions can weaken a cat's immune system, making them more susceptible to upper respiratory infections. Additionally, if a cat is already carrying a virus, stress from the cold weather can trigger a flare-up of symptoms like sneezing or congestion.
